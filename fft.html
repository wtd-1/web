History

  • During the Cold War there was an interest in the U.S. government to detect seismic activity.
  • In theory this should be simple:
    1. Measure the ground vibrations;
    2. Convert them into electrical signals;
    3. Record 2 as seismograms.
  • 2 is the most computationally expensive part.

Nowadays

  • The Fourier Transform (and its speedier younger sibling) is obviously applied beyond reconnaissance.
  • Digital Recording and pitch correction
  • Telecommunications (e.g., 5G)
  • Data compression (e.g., perceptual compression in audio and images)
  • Pattern recognition and filtering
  • In place of the self-attention sublayer in a Transformer encoder [FNet 2021]
    • 70% faster TPU and 80% faster GPU training

Demonstrating the Fourier Transform

Visualising the Fourier Transform: Foundations

  • The Fourier transform takes signals from the time domain and converts them into the frequency domain.
  • Before it, one had to convolve the input signal $x$ with a system's impulse response $h$ to realise an output $y$.
    • For a linear time-invariant system, that is $y[n] = \sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty} x[m]\,h[n-m].$
    • $n$ is the time-step at which $y$ is computed.
    • $m$ is a "dummy variable" that parameterises the input samples.
    • The system's response to a single impulse at time $m$ is the shifted impulse response $h[n-m]$.
    • I.e., $x[m]$ is the weight, how large the input sample at time $m$ is and $h[n-m]$ is the response delay, how much of that sample's response is still showing $n-m$ steps after the sample arrived.
  • Really, the Fourier transform is a change of basis where we have:
    1. the standard basis (the time domain representation), and
    2. the Fourier basis (the frequency domain representation).
  • 2 is better stated as the basis of complex exponentials
  • A complex exponential is the function $\theta \mapsto e^{i\theta}$ with $\theta \in \mathbb{R}$: it takes an angle $\theta$ to a point on the unit circle in the complex plane.
  • Key Idea 1: Rather than a "values over time" representation, a signal can be seen as coefficients of oscillatory basis functions, $$1, e^{2\pi ix}, e^{4\pi ix}, \dots, e^{2\pi ikx}$$
  • These coefficients are numbers $c_k$ in the signal we are representing, $f(x)=\Sigma_k c_k e^{2\pi ikx}$.
    • Each $c_k$ is a complex number that states how many oscillations $k$ a signal contains.
    • Incidentally each $c_k$ has a size ($|c_k|$) that tells us the amplitude and an angle ($\arg c_k$) that tells us the phase in the complex plane.
    • E.g., suppose $c_1 = 1-i$. The subscript says this is the coefficient of the wave with $k=1$, namely $e^{2\pi i x}$, the amplitude is $\sqrt{2}$ since $|1-i|=\sqrt{2}$, and the angle is $-\pi/4$ radians.

" With Euler's Identity

  • Key Idea 2: Euler's Identity undergirds the Fourier transform. It is $e^{i\theta} = \cos \theta + i\sin \theta$
  • With it, we can rewrite sinusoidal functions in terms of complex exponentials, $$\cos \theta = \frac{e^{i \theta} + e^{-i\theta}}{2}, \quad \sin \theta = \frac{e^{i \theta} - e^{-i\theta}}{2i}.$$

From the Fourier Transform to the Discrete Fourier Transform

  • So far the signal $f$ has been a function of continuous $x$, and each coefficient $c_k$ comes from an integral over $x$.
  • A computer stores a finite list of $n$ samples $a_0, \dots, a_{n-1}$, not a continuous function.
  • The discrete Fourier transform finds the frequency coefficients from those $n$ samples alone, using a finite sum and this takes $\Theta(n)$ time.
  • The Fast Fourier Transform is an algorithm that computes the discrete Fourier transform exactly, in $\Theta(n\log n)$ time.

Note: from here on, "Fourier transform" means the discrete one, on a length-$n$ vector over a ring $R.$

Mathematical Foundations

  • A ring is a nonempty set $R$ endowed with the binary operations of addition ($+$) and multiplication ($\cdot$). It satisfies the following properties.
    1. $R$ is associative and commutative over addition and contains an element $0 \in R$ that provides $R$'s additive identity (i.e., $R$ is an additive abelian group);
    2. $R$ is associative over multiplication and $1 \in R$ gives $R$'s multiplicative identity (i.e., $R$ is a multiplicative monoid);
    3. Multiplcation is distributive with respect to addition.

Defining the Fourier Transform

  • The Fourier transform is defined over an arbitrary commutative ring $(R, +, \cdot, 0, 1)$ in the complex numbers ($\mathbb{C}$); i.e., it is defined over a ring $R$ where addition and multiplication are commutative and are equipped with their respective identities.
  • This is actually quite useful since $n$ is an exact power of two in this field of complex numbers.
  • $\exists \omega \in R$ called the principal $n$th root of unity s.t.:
    •
    1. $\omega \neq 1$;
    2. $\omega^n = 1$;
    3. $\Sigma_{j=0}^{n-1}\omega^{jp} = 0$ for $1 \leq p < n$.
      • Pick any $p$ with $1 \leq p < n$. Write the $n$ powers $\omega^{0\cdot p}, \omega^{1\cdot p}, \dots, \omega^{(n-1)p}$ and add all of them, including the first, which is $\omega^{0}=1$. The total is $0$. E.g., $n=4,$ $\omega=i$, $p=1$: $1 + i + (-1) + (-i) = 0$.
  • The elements $\omega^0, \omega^1, \dots, \omega^{n-1}$ are the $n$th roots of unity.
  • Incidentally a root of unity is any complex number that is 1 once raised to some $n \in \mathbb{Z}^+$.
    • $e^{2\pi i /n}$ where $i = \sqrt{-1}$ is a principal $n$th root of unity in the ring of complex numbers.
  • Being principal drives the discrete Fourier transform: the sums that vanish in condition 3 are exactly what let the inverse transform recover the input.

The discrete Fourier transform

  • Let $R$ be a commutative ring with a principal $n$th root of unity $\omega$, and let $\mathbf{a} = [a_0, a_1, \dots, a_{n-1}]^T \in R^n$.
  • The Fourier matrix is the $n \times n$ matrix $A$ over $R$ with $A[i,j] = \omega^{ij}$ for $0 \leq i, j < n.$
  • The discrete Fourier transform of $\mathbf{a}$ is the vector $F(\mathbf{a}) = A\mathbf{a}$, with components $$b_i = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} a_k\,\omega^{ik}, \qquad 0 \leq i < n.$$
  • If $n$ has a multiplicative inverse $n^{-1}$ in $R$, then $A$ is invertible with $A^{-1}[i,j] = n^{-1}\omega^{-ij}$, and $F^{-1}(\mathbf{b}) = A^{-1}\mathbf{b}$ is the inverse discrete Fourier transform.
  • Obviously $F^{-1}F(\mathbf{a}) = \mathbf{a}$

Here $n$ also denotes $1 + 1 + \cdots + 1$ ($n$ terms) in $R$.

Evaluation and Interpolation

  • Let $p(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} a_k x^k$. Its coefficient representation is the vector $\mathbf{a}=[a_0,\dots,a_{n-1}]^T$.
  • Its value representation is the list of values $p(x_0),\dots,p(x_{n-1})$ at $n$ distinct points.
  • Evaluation goes from coefficients to values. Interpolation goes back from values (at the $n$th roots of unity) to coefficients.
  • The $i$th component of the discrete Fourier transform is $b_i=\sum_k a_k\omega^{ik}=p(\omega^i)$: the transform evaluates $p$ at $\omega^0,\dots,\omega^{n-1}$, and the inverse transform interpolates the values $b_0,\dots,b_{n-1}$ back into the coefficients $a_0,\dots,a_{n-1}$ of $p$.

The Matrix form of the DFT

Theorem 1 (Uniqueness of an Interpolating Polynomial). For any set ${(x_0,y_0), (x_1,y_1), \dots, (x_{n-1}, y_{n-1})}$ of $n$ point-value pairs such that all the $x_k$ values are distinct, there is a unique polynomial $A(x)$ of degree-bound $n$ such taht $y_k=A(x_k)$ for $k = 0, 1, \dots, n-1.$

  • This theorem uses a special kind of matrix in its proof.

    • Proof (very rough sketch). The $n$ equations $y_k=A(x_k)$ form one linear system $V\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{y}$, where $V$ is a Vandermonde matrix at the nodes $x_k$. Because the $x_k$ are distinct, $V$ is invertible, so $\mathbf{a}=V^{-1}\mathbf{y}$ exists and is unique. $\quad \quad \blacksquare$

The Fourier matrix is a Vandermonde matrix, namely the one whose nodes are the $n$th roots of unity. The reverse is false, because a Vandermonde matrix at other nodes is not a Fourier matrix.

  • The fact this matrix form exists means we can observe the discrete Fourier transform as a matrix-vector multiplication given by $F(\mathbf{a}) = A\mathbf{a}$.
  • Explicitly, with $A[i,j]=\omega^{ij}$, $$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 & \cdots & 1 \\ 1 & \omega & \omega^{2} & \cdots & \omega^{n-1} \\ 1 & \omega^{2} & \omega^{4} & \cdots & \omega^{2(n-1)} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 1 & \omega^{n-1} & \omega^{2(n-1)} & \cdots & \omega^{(n-1)^{2}} \end{bmatrix} $$
  • Thus the discrete Fourier transform is a linear transformation.

The Fast Fourier Transform: Entrance

  • Computing the DFT (and its inverse) is a $O(n^2)$ process.
  • We can achieve a much better $\Theta(n\log n)$ computation by...
    1. exploiting the similarities among the portions of the $n$ sums applied by $A\mathbf{a}$, or
    2. splitting the input into even and odd indices.
  • Both are equivalent because both use divide-and-conquer and rely on the property of repeating powers of $\omega$.
  • We will proceed with expressing the FFT as in 2.

Lemmas of the FFT: Cancellation

The Cancellation Lemma. For any integers $n>0, k\geq 0,$ and $d>0$, $\omega^{dk}_{dn} = \omega^k_n.$

Proof. Recall the $n$th principal root of unity being $\omega_n = e^{2\pi i/n}.$ By expansion, we see the following. $$ \begin{aligned} \omega_{dn}^{dk} &= \left(e^{2\pi i/(dn)}\right)^{dk} \\ &\class{fragment}{{}= e^{2\pi i\, dk/(dn)}} \\ &\class{fragment}{{}= e^{2\pi i\, k/n}} \\ &\class{fragment}{{}= \left(e^{2\pi i/n}\right)^{k}} \\ &\class{fragment}{{}= \omega_n^{k}. \quad \quad \blacksquare} \end{aligned} $$

The Cancellation Corollary. For any even integer $n>0$, $\omega^{n/2}_n = w_2 = -1.$

Proof. Recall again the $n$th principal root of unity. Also recall Euler's Identity as $e^{i\theta} = \cos \theta + i\sin \theta.$ $$ \begin{aligned} \omega_n^{n/2} &= \left(e^{2\pi i/n}\right)^{n/2} \\ &\class{fragment}{{}= e^{2\pi i\,(n/2)/n}} \\ &\class{fragment}{{}= e^{\pi i\, n/n}} \\ &\class{fragment}{{}= e^{\pi i}} \\ &\class{fragment}{{}= \cos\pi + i\sin\pi} \\ &\class{fragment}{{}= -1. \quad \quad \blacksquare} \end{aligned} $$

Lemmas of the FFT: Halving

The Halving Lemma. If $n>0$ is even, then the squares of the $n$ complex $n$th roots of unity are the $n/2$ complex $(n/2)$th roots of unity.

Proof. Recall the result of the Cancellation Corollary. $\omega^{n/2}_n = w_2 = -1$ implies $\omega^{k+(n/2)}_n = -\omega^{k}_n$, so squaring gives the following. $$ \begin{aligned} \left(\omega_n^{k+(n/2)}\right)^2 &= \left(\omega_n^{k} \times \omega_n^{n/2}\right)^2 \\ &\class{fragment}{{}= \left(\omega_n^{k} \times (-1)\right)^2} \\ &\class{fragment}{{}= \left(-\omega_n^{k}\right)^2} \\ &\class{fragment}{{}= \left(\omega_n^{k}\right)^2.} \end{aligned} $$ So $\omega_n^{k}$ and $\omega_n^{k+(n/2)}$ have the same square. By the Cancellation Lemma with $d = 2$, $$ \begin{aligned} \left(\omega_n^{k}\right)^2 &= \omega_n^{2k} \\ &\class{fragment}{{}= \omega_{n/2}^{k}.} \end{aligned} $$ This means as $k$ runs over $0, 1, \ldots, n-1$, the squares give each $(n/2)$th root of unity $\omega_{n/2}^{k}$ exactly twice. $\quad \quad \blacksquare$

Lemmas of the FFT: Summation

Geometric series. For real $x \neq 1$, the summation $$\sum_{k=0}^{n} x^k = 1 + x + x^2 + \cdots + x^n$$ is a geometric series and has the value $$\sum_{k=0}^{n} x^k = \frac{x^{n+1} - 1}{x - 1}.$$

The Summation Lemma. For any integer $n \geq 1$ and nonzero integer $k$ not divisible by $n,$ $$\sum_{j=0}^{n-1} \left(\omega_n^k\right)^j = 0.$$

Proof. The geometric series applies to complex values as well as to those real, giving $$ \begin{aligned} \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} \left(\omega_n^k\right)^j &= \frac{\left(\omega_n^k\right)^n - 1}{\omega_n^k - 1} \\ &\class{fragment}{{}= \frac{\left(\omega_n^n\right)^k - 1}{\omega_n^k - 1}} \\ &\class{fragment}{{}= \frac{(1)^k - 1}{\omega_n^k - 1}} \\ &\class{fragment}{{}= 0. \quad \quad \blacksquare} \end{aligned} $$

The denominator is not 0 since $\omega_n^k = 1$ only when $k$ is divisible by $n$, which the lemma statement prohibits.

The FFT: on stage

  • Split the input $A(x)$ into an $A^{even}(x)$ and an $A^{odd}$ of degree bound $n/2$ where $$ \begin{aligned} &\class{fragment}{\llap{A^{even}} = a_0 + a_2 x + a_4 x^2 + \dots + a_{n-2} x^{n/2 - 1} ,} \\ &\class{fragment}{\llap{A^{odd}} = a_1 + a_3 x + \dots + a_{n-1}x^{n/2-1} .} \end{aligned} $$
  • If we wanted to stitch $A(x)$ back together then we can use $A(x) = A^{even}(x^2) + xA^{odd}(x^2).$
    • Example with $n=4$: $A(x)=a_0+a_1x+a_2x^2+a_3x^3.$ $$ \begin{aligned} A(x) &= (a_0 + a_2x^2) + (a_1x + a_3x^3) \\ &\class{fragment}{{}= (a_0 + a_2x^2) + x\,(a_1 + a_3x^2)} \\ &\class{fragment}{{}= A^{even}(x^2) + x\,A^{odd}(x^2),} \end{aligned} $$ where $A^{even}(y)=a_0+a_2y$ and $A^{odd}(y)=a_1+a_3y.$
    • Factoring out $x$ turns the odd exponents into even ones, since $x^3 = x\cdot x^2$. Both groups are then polynomials in $x^2$.
    • At $x=\omega_n^k$ we get $x^2=\omega_n^{2k}=\omega_{n/2}^{k}$, an $(n/2)$th root of unity (cancellation lemma).
    • Since $\omega_n^{k+n/2}=-\omega_n^k$, the points $\omega_n^k$ and $\omega_n^{k+n/2}$ have the same square, so only $n/2$ distinct values of $x^2$ occur.

The FFT: Backstage

  • The process just observed is evaluating the original polynomial $A(x)$ at $\omega^0_n, \omega^1_n, \dots, \omega^{n-1}_n.$
  • But instead of drawing up a Fourier matrix, all we did was:
    1. evalute the degree-bound $n/2$ polynomials at points $(\omega^0_n)^2, (\omega^1_n)^2, \dots, (\omega^{n-1}_n)^2$ and then
    2. combine the results.
  • The "[f]actoring out $x$" from the previous slide means we are keeping the form of the original problem.
  • The FFT is recursively evaluating the $A^{even}$ and $A^{odd}$ splits, of degree-bound $n/2$, at the $n/2$ complex $n/2$ roots of unity.
  • Altogether, what was a $\text{DFT}_n$ computation decomposed into two $\text{DFT}_{n/2}$ ones.
  • In the end, the running time is $\Theta(n\log n)$, which can be found via the recurrence relation $T(n) = 2T(n/2) + \Theta(n)$ where $2T(n/2)$ counts the two recursive invocations, each on an input of length $n/2$, and $\Theta(n)$ is the work done in one invocation outside those recursive calls.

FFT in the Wild: Caracal

  • Caracal replaces self-attention in a language model with an FFT-based module [Caracal 2026].
  • The problem: attention rebuilds token $t$ from all $L$ tokens $v_0, \dots, v_{L-1}$, $$r_t = \sum_{j=0}^{L-1} \alpha_{tj}\, v_j, \qquad 0 \leq t < L.$$ There are $L \times L$ weights $\alpha_{tj}$, so the cost is $O(L^2)$.
  • A language model is also causal: output $t$ may use only $v_0, \dots, v_t$. Attention enforces this with a mask that sets $\alpha_{tj} = 0$ for $j > t$.
  • The discrete Fourier transform has the same weighted-sum shape, $r_t = \sum_{j} \omega^{tj} v_j$, but the FFT never forms the weights, so there is nothing to mask.
  • One FFT per prefix would work, but that is $L$ transforms and $O(L^2 \log L)$ in total, which is worse than attention.

FFT in the Wild: Caracal's fix

  • Write the causal sum as a convolution: $r_t = \sum_{j=0}^{t} v_j\, g_{t-j}$ which is familiar to us as $$y[n] = \sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty} x[m]\,h[n-m]$$ with $x = v$, $h = g$, $y = r$, $n = t$, and $m = j$. Both $x$ and $h$ are $0$ at negative indices, so only $0 \leq m \leq n$ contributes.
  • Recall that convolution is polynomial multiplication: let $X(u)=\sum_m x[m]\, u^m$ and $H(u)=\sum_k h[k]\, u^k$, with the samples as coefficients. The coefficient of $u^n$ in $X(u)\,H(u)$ is $\sum_{m+k=n} x[m]\,h[k] = y[n].$
  • So we can use evaluation and interpolation:
    1. evaluate $X$ and $H$ at the roots of unity $\omega^0, \dots, \omega^{n-1}$ (FFT);
    2. multiply the values pointwise, $X(\omega^i)\,H(\omega^i)$, in $O(n)$ time;
    3. interpolate the product's coefficients (inverse FFT).
  • Each transform costs $O(n \log n)$, where $n$ is the padded length, so the total is $O(L \log L)$ instead of $O(L^2)$.

In Caracal the kernel $h$ is computed from the input by a small network, so the mixing depends on content.

FFT in the Wild: Why Caracal pads

  • At the $n$th roots of unity $u=\omega^i$, $u^n = 1$, since $(\omega^i)^n=(\omega^n)^i=1$, so a term of degree $n + k$ wraps onto degree $k$.
  • Wrapping lets a later token "leak" into an earlier output. With $L = n = 2$, $$y'[0] = x[0]\,h[0] + x[1]\,h[1],$$ so $x[1]$, a future token, changes output $0$.
  • The fix is pad, FFT, multiply, inverse FFT, truncate procedure where we:
    1. pad $x$ and $h$ with zeros to length $n = 2L$;
    2. transform, multiply pointwise, and transform back;
    3. keep only the first $L$ outputs.
  • The product has degree at most $2L-2$, below $n = 2L$, so nothing wraps into the outputs we keep.
  • At a context length of 8192, the paper reports training nearly three times faster than a Llama baseline.
